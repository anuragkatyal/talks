The Activities Were Memorable.
The Formulas Were Not.

Paper folding, three formulas, and the non trivial distance between a lesson that goes well and a lesson that works.

Anurag Katyal Intermediate Algebra Fall 2025, Spring 2026
Fifteen minutes. The talk is about three activities I built, why they looked like they worked, and what I found afterwards in my own source code.
THE ASSUMPTION

A physical model makes a symbolic identity unforgetable.

State the bet plainly, because the rest of the talk is about how the bet failed. Nobody in this room disagrees with the premise, which is the problem.
Evidence, during class

By every measure I was watching, the lessons worked

Engagement

Full participation, side conversations about the mathematics, students comparing folds.

Model clarity

Nobody was confused by the geometry. The pictures did what pictures are supposed to do.

In-activity correctness

Answer boxes were filled correctly, step after step, all the way to the formula.

Explanation on request

Asked why the formula held, students pointed at the paper and said something true.

These are the measures I was using in the moment, and they were all positive. Say out loud that every one of them was collected during the activity.
The assessment

What the three questions asked for

01

Straightforward application of the formulas.

02

Find the missing factor given one factor and the expanded product.

03

Scenario where they have to side with one of two students in a story and defend their choice.

Three question types, in increasing distance from the activity. The first is closest to what they practiced. The third asks them to judge someone else's reasoning and say why.

What came back

The errors the activities were built to prevent

(a + b)² = a² + b²

(a − b)² = a² − b²

(a + b)(a − b) = a² − 2ab + b²

Written, in some cases, by students whose folds and labels had been correct a week earlier.

Read the three lines out loud. These are not new errors; they are the exact errors the activities were built to prevent.
Part two

Some issues, all in my own source code

Pivot. This part of the talk is about my design rather than about students. Everything ahead comes from reading my own source code.
Mechanism 01

The activity wrote the algebra; students recognized it

Square+ception, Step 3

What is the area of region 1 and 2?

a² ab b² a² + ab a² + ab + ab a² + ab + b² a² + ab + ab + b²
This is the strongest of the five. Read the option list out loud and let the room notice that the answer to each question is the previous one plus a term.
Mechanism 02

The activities were error-suppressing by design

hide="not $firstDim.creditAchieved=1"
All three activities use this. In the sum activity it hides entire sections, so the lesson is a waterfall: you cannot see step three while step two is wrong.
Mechanism 03

The misconception appeared only as a distractor

Square+ception, Step 3, item 5

What does a² + ab + ab + b² simplify to?

a² + a²b² + b² a² + 2ab + b² a² + 2a²b² + b² a² + b² (a² + b²)²
Point at a squared plus b squared in the option list. That is the error on the assessment, and this is the only time in the lesson a student is asked to think about it.
Mechanism 04

The practice trained filling, not choosing

Two Squares

Square+ception
{()}^2+2{()}{()}+{()}^2
Square-ception

Each activity's practice set contains only that activity's formula, and the template is already in the box. Selection is the skill the test measures and the one the practice removed.
Thoughts from a group

What they said while they were folding

Student A

This is so much more fun than actual math.

Student B

This is just folding and ripping up paper. Of course it's more fun than math.

Student C

Nah there's a bit of geometry. I remember it from high school. I don't get why we are doing this in an Algebra class though.

One group, overheard in sequence. Read them in order and let the third one land. The activity registered as a break from math rather than as math, and one student said so directly.
In closing

The activity was a demonstration. It was not an argument.
